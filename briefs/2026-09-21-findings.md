# Summary {-}

The platform asks whether a mine can reorder its own seismic processing queue
by what it knows about where events are and who is near them — so that the
work that matters to people is done first — and what that costs in SLA
breaches and cloud capacity. Every scaling decision comes from the real
autoscaler, driven through its simulation adapter; every workload replays
identically from its seed; every number below is tied to the commits that
produced it (Appendix A).

> **What has been found so far.**
>
> 1. **Intent's value follows contention.** On a calibrated day with a medium
>    earthquake, decaying work that endangers no one cut breaches by 27 % and
>    the wait for the first location of events that exposed someone by 57 %, at
>    no extra cloud time. On a quiet workday the same setting is a net cost —
>    breaches rose from 49 to 1,160. With scarce cloud capacity it also saves a
>    third of the cloud bill; with plenty, it hardly matters.
> 2. **Restored work must not buy cloud.** Decayed work that comes back is
>    already late; counted as urgent it costs 41 % more cloud time. Exempted,
>    decay cuts breaches by a quarter at 4 % *less* cloud.
> 3. **Decayed work needs a level of its own.** Decaying into a level that
>    also carries submitted work made endangering events finish *later* than
>    with no intent at all. Below every submitted level they finish five to
>    seven times sooner. This is now the default (simlab-api 3.0.0).
> 4. **Location quality is the largest remaining lever.** An oracle that knows
>    where events really were locates endangering events up to 56 % sooner. The
>    allowance for location error is the dial between relaxing work and missing
>    endangering events, and a fixed allowance fails once pick error grows:
>    at 20 ms, nearly half of endangering events are decayed at some point.
> 5. **Promotion buys finished locations at a price no variant tested avoids.**
>    It finishes endangering events six to fourteen times sooner, but costs
>    breaches and cloud time and, under estimates, delays their first
>    location. Acting before a location exists, with a 30-sensor array,
>    helps nothing and makes promotion far worse.
> 6. **The platform is sound where it has been tested, and was not always.**
>    System experiments found four defects; two had moved published numbers,
>    one in the controller's favour. Each is now guarded by a unit test where
>    the code lives, and a run recorded from committed builds replays
>    identically from its provenance alone.

The practical conclusion is that intent should be switched on by contention
rather than left on: the platform can already change intent mid-run, and it
pays only under a burst or an earthquake. What should decide it, and an
allowance for location error that follows each location's own uncertainty,
are the next questions (Section 6).

# What was built, and how it is measured

## The platform

The **autoscaler** decides capacity by simulating the queue forward, not by a
threshold: it searches for the smallest executor count with no predicted
deadline breach, fills the on-premise tier to its cap, and sends only proven
overflow to cloud. It models the coldstart of new capacity (finding 003) and
says in words why it chose each count.

**Simlab** replays seeded seismic workloads through that autoscaler. Its
queue simulator owns queue mechanics and the autoscaler owns scaling
decisions; neither knows how the other works, and the only substitution is the
autoscaler's platform adapter. A simulated run therefore tests the real
decision engine rather than a model of it.

## Intent

Intent is the mine using what it knows to reorder work already queued. The
protected volume is the union, over every person and vehicle, of where each
was when an event happened, where it is now, and where its planned route takes
it over a **lookahead** (300 s by default). An event threatens that volume
when the ground motion it can cause reaches it, using the scaling law of the
Canadian Rockburst Support Handbook, `ppv = C*·√(10^(mN+1))/R`, with the design
constant C\* = 0.25 m²/s; levels are moderate above 0.01 m/s, high above 0.1
and very high above 1. Each zone is widened by an **allowance for location
error** (±50 m by default, the re-entry exclusion practice figure).

| Mode | What it does |
|---|---|
| **decay** | Work for an event whose moderate zone reaches no one drops below other work. The default, because it makes nothing more urgent. |
| **promote** | Work for an event whose high zone reaches someone rises to the top level — under estimates, once the event is located. |
| **both** | Both at once. |
| **restore** | Decayed work returns to its submitted level when something protected comes within reach (on by default). |
| **burst exemption** | Decayed, promoted or restored work may not be the reason cloud is bought; it is still served, and still counted when late. |
| **knowledge** | `estimate` judges each event from the mine's own first location (four processed picks); `truth` is an oracle that knows where events were. |

Table: The intent settings the sweeps vary. `docs/intent.md` in simlab-api has the full list.

## What is measured

| Measure | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Breaches | Jobs that waited longer than the deadline of the level they were served at. |
| As submitted | Breaches against the level each job *arrived* at — what intent cost the SLA work was submitted under. |
| Cloud h | Cloud executor-hours of ready capacity: the billed tier. |
| Locate exposing | Seconds from an event to its first location, for events that truly exposed someone to at least moderate ground motion (judged by the simulator from the truth). The operator-facing measure. |
| Process exposing | Seconds until every pick of such an event is processed — its final location. |
| Exposing decayed | Of those events, how many intent decayed at some point: the misses. |

Table: Measures. Figures are means over seeds; the reports give the range across seeds.

## Scenarios

- **The two-hour burst** (eight sweeps, 540 runs, six seeds): a 1.6 km mine with
  30 sensors and 90 background events an hour; one rock burst at 30 minutes
  that raises the event rate 45-fold and decays over 30 minutes, with a main
  shock of Nuttli magnitude 2.5; 12 on-premise and 60 cloud executors.
  Built on autoscaler 1.1.0 and simlab-api 2.0.0.
- **Three calibrated days** (one sweep, 48 runs, four seeds): a mine fitted to
  an operational processing catalogue (Section 3), over 24 hours — an ordinary
  workday of about 40,000 jobs, the same day with a rock burst (55,000), and
  with a medium earthquake of Nuttli magnitude 4 and a six-hour aftershock tail
  (93,000). Built on simlab-api 2.0.1.

On the workday the fleet keeps up on its own — 49 breaches in a day — so

first location of exposing events by 57 %, at 1 % less cloud time.

## Restored work must not buy cloud

Decay alone is not free. Decayed work that is **restored** — because a vehicle
is now heading towards its event — returns to its submitted level having waited
all along, and with deadlines measured from arrival it is often already late.
To the autoscaler a waiting job that is already late is a breach no capacity
avoids, so it runs flat out.

```table
from: reports/intent-modes
section: Against off · estimate
rows: ["decay · estimate", "decay, restored exempt · estimate", "decay, clock restarts · estimate", "decay, final · estimate"]
rename: {"decay · estimate": "Decay, restores counted", "decay, restored exempt · estimate": "Decay, restored work exempt (default)", "decay, clock restarts · estimate": "Decay, deadline restarts on a change", "decay, final · estimate": "Decay made final (no restore)"}
caption: Four ways of handling restored work, against no intent on the two-hour burst (seed totals over six seeds). From `reports/intent-modes`.
```

Counting restores buys the ordering at 41 % more cloud time. Exempting restored
work from cloud burst keeps a quarter fewer breaches at 4 % less cloud, locates
exposing events 13 % sooner, finishes them four times sooner (5,025 to 1,113 s)
and leaves the SLA as submitted unchanged — the work decayed was work that
could wait. That is the default.

**Decay made final** has the fewest breaches of any estimate arm, but it stops
protecting anyone who arrives after the lookahead. Exposure is judged at the
moment of an event, so nothing measured here can see that cost; it is why
final decay is not the default, and a measure for it is an open thread.

## Decayed work needs a level of its own

The calibrated job mix submits a quarter of its work at priority 0 — the level
decayed work used to go to. The two decay arms in Figure 1 differ only in that.


On the rock-burst day, exposing events finished in 6,421 s with no intent,
9,048 s decaying to level 0, and 1,766 s decaying below every submitted level;
under the earthquake, 49,439 s, 46,657 s and 7,154 s. simlab-api 3.0.0 now
decays below the floor by default, and both arms are on the record.

The eight two-hour sweeps are not wrong on this, but they were lucky: their
job mix submitted nothing below priority 25, so level 0 was already below
everything and the collision could not appear. A job mix taken from a real
pipeline found it in its first run.

## Knowing where events really were is worth a great deal

With the oracle's knowledge, decay locates exposing events in 38 s rather than
87 s (−56 %) and cuts breaches by 88 % (95 % if made final). Across six seeds
129 events truly exposed someone to at least moderate ground motion; the
estimate arms decayed two or three of them per seed at some point, the oracle
none. The distance between estimate and oracle is what better locations could
still win, and the next two sweeps ask how.


| Allowance | Breaches vs no intent | Exposing events decayed per seed |
|---|---:|---:|
| ±0 m | −47 % | 5.7–8.5 |
| ±50 m (default) | −25 % | 2.3–3.3 |
| ±150 m | −4 % | 0.8–1.0 |

Table: The allowance, across lookaheads (ranges are over the four lookaheads), from the report's reading.

No allowance relaxes the most and decays a quarter to two-fifths of the events
that truly exposed someone; ±150 m protects nearly all of them and relaxes
almost nothing. The lookahead changes breaches and cloud time by at most a few
per cent. It reduces misses where the allowance leaves room for them (at ±0 m,
8.5 per seed with no lookahead against 5.7 at fifteen minutes), and five
minutes is not obviously wrong.

## A fixed allowance fails as locations get worse

As pick error grows, decay cuts *more* breaches — because worse locations put
more events outside everyone's zone, including events that were not:

```chart

kind: lines

where: {intent: "decay, restored exempt"}
names: {"jitter 0 ms": "0", "jitter 5 ms": "5", "jitter 20 ms": "20", "jitter 50 ms": "50"}
xlabel: pick error, ms

  - {measure: ttp_exposing_mean_s, label: Seconds to finish exposing events, values: true}
caption: "Falling breaches here are not good news. At 20 ms of pick error — about 116 m of travel in rock — the ±50 m allowance no longer covers location error and nearly half of the 21.5 exposing events per seed are decayed at some point; at 50 ms, two-thirds. From `reports/pick-jitter`."
```

The oracle is untouched by pick error by construction. What this asks for is
an allowance tied to each location's own uncertainty — its residual, or the
spread of solutions that fit its picks — rather than one number for every
event. Until then, the allowance should be set for the worst pick error a mine
expects, not the typical one.

## Promotion buys finished locations, at a price

Promotion raises the work of located events that could expose someone to high
ground motion. Under estimates it can act only once an event is located, so it
cannot speed the location that decides it; and the promoted work, already past
its new 30-second deadline, takes capacity from everything else.

```table
rename: {"decay, restored exempt · to 400 · at high · from arrival": "Decay alone, for comparison", "both · to 400 · at high · from arrival": "Promote to the top level", "both · to 100 · at high · from arrival": "Promote less high (to 100)", "both · to 400 · at high · from the change": "Promote, deadline restarts on promotion", "both · to 400 · at very-high · from the change": "Promote only at very high motion (never fires)"}
caption: Variants of promotion (with decay) against no intent on the two-hour burst. From `reports/promotion`.
```

- Every high-level promotion finishes exposing events in 500–550 s, against
  1,113 s with decay alone and 5,025 s with no intent — and costs about a fifth
  more cloud time and a quarter more breaches as submitted.
- Restarting the deadline on promotion removes the extra breaches but not the
  cloud time. Promoting less high changes little.
- Very high ground motion needs about magnitude 2.9 at the source and the
  largest main shock here is 2.5, so promotion at that level never fires; those
  arms are decay alone. Even under the calibrated earthquake it reaches only
  one or two events a day.
- Switched on at the burst, promotion costs what it costs throughout; switched
  off again thirty minutes later it recovers only part (Table 3): the promoted
  work is already late and already dispatched.
- Under the calibrated earthquake, promotion's first locations were also better
  than no intent (−44 %), at 12 % more breaches and 8 % more cloud time.

If finished locations of endangering events are the goal, promotion delivers
them; if SLA and cost are, decay does better. Promotion that raises only the
next few picks an event needs, rather than all its remaining work, is untested.

## Acting before a location, with a sparse array, does not help

With thirty sensors across a 1.6 km mine the fourth sensor to trigger is
hundreds of metres from the first, and that spread is the allowance: at any
assumed magnitude the zone reaches someone, so nothing is decayed before it is
located and decay with and without pre-location is indistinguishable.
Promotion from the same footing promotes nearly everything on arrival:

```table

caption: Pre-location against no intent. Breaches rise nearly sixfold (1,687 to 9,665) when promotion acts on the first sensors to trigger. From `reports/pre-location`.
```

A denser array near active workings, or a spread measured from arrival-time
differences rather than sensor distances, would be needed first.

## More people protect more of the mine


A larger crew protects more of the mine, so less work is decayed and the
breaches decay saves shrink (−28 % to −17 %), while misses grow with the number
of exposing events (16.5, 21.5 and 37 per seed) at around one in ten.
Promotion gets worse as the crew grows, because more events are near someone.
Crews move on their own routes, so a larger crew is a different set of routes,
not the same routes with more people on them.

# Calibrating to an operational pipeline

The calibrated mine is fitted to an extract of a real seismic-processing
pipeline — process records of its `pick`, `associate` and `locate` jobs. The
extract stays outside every repository and no row of it is quoted; what
follows are aggregates (`docs/calibration.md`, `docs/workflow-inference.md`).

| | |
|---|---|
| Dense window | 29 days, 1,087,951 records |
| Jobs on a typical day | 39,162 (median) |
| Jobs an hour | 1,644 on average; busiest hour 1.19×, quietest 0.63× |
| Job mix | `locate` 51.0 %, `pick` 26.7 %, `associate` 22.2 % |
| Execution time, mean | `locate` 47.2 s, `pick` 14.6 s, `associate` 1.3 s; weighted 28.3 s |
| Priority | static per type: `associate` 100, `locate` 50, `pick` 0 |
| Waiting time | median 0.03 s — the pipeline never contended |

Table: What the extract says, in aggregate.

Simlab derives one pick job per triggered sensor per event, so with 30 sensors
matching 1,644 jobs an hour needs about 91 events an hour; a generated day
carries 39,000–40,300 jobs, within about 2 % of the measured day. The extract
fixes only the arrival side. Deadlines, the SLA and contention are the
platform's own, induced by capping capacity, and the fit does not carry the
daily swing (±35 % in the model against +19 %/−37 %), the spread of execution
times across three job types, or the arrival mechanism.

That mechanism was then measured over 672 hours of the extract:

- **`associate` is a timer.** Sweeps run every 10.0 s (98.8 % of gaps within
  half a second), each over a 30-minute window, and 84 % of them produce
  nothing.
- **A sweep emits its own locates.** All 555,153 locates were submitted during
  the execution of a sweep, a median of 2.4 s after it was submitted, so the
  pick–associate–locate dependency is visible in timing even though no record
  links them.
- **A sweep's cost follows what it emits**: about 1.13 s + 0.08 s per locate.
  Sweeping half as often does not halve the work, which a flat per-sweep cost
  would wrongly suggest.
- **Completed picks do not predict a productive sweep**, so an event-driven
  associate is a design to measure, not a reconstruction of production.
- **About 27 candidate events an hour**, against the calibrated mine's 91. Both
  are right: 91 is a job-count parameter, because simlab derives picks from
  events while production batches up to 20 seismograms per pick job whether or
  not anything seismic happened.

An earlier pass reported the opposite of the second point by measuring each
locate against the previous sweep's end rather than its own sweep's submission;
the tell was a lag with a floor of exactly one timer interval. It stays on the
record because a pipeline simulator's comparisons are only as good as the
baseline they are measured against.

# What the experiments found in the platform itself

Five experiments test properties only the assembled system has: real binaries,
a real database, services genuinely talking to each other. Four defects were
found, by them and by reading the code against its documentation. Each was
confirmed by reintroducing it, and each fix is guarded by a fast unit test in
the repository that owns the code.

| Finding | Where | Found by | What it did to results |
|---|---|---|---|
| **001** Work in progress dropped on every scale-down | simlab-api queue | Experiment 002 | A dropped job never completes and never breaches, so it flattered the controller: 13 of 14,288 jobs lost under tight caps. |
| **002** Autonomous targets never scaled again after a restart | autoscaler registry | Experiment 001 | A silent outage of the service's primary function on real infrastructure; no simulated result affected. |
| **003** The decision engine never read its configured coldstart | autoscaler policy | Reading the code | Decisions assumed requested capacity worked at once — optimistic exactly during a ramp. |
| **004** Breaches counted at the wrong moments | simlab-api queue | A property test | Every breach count before simlab-api 2.0.0 is off, in both directions; comparisons within a version stood. |

Table: Defects found. Each finding in `findings/` says what happened, why nothing caught it, and what guards it now.

The more useful record is *why nothing caught them*, because each was a class
of blind spot rather than a slip:

- **A round-trip test that names fields can only catch the fields someone
  remembered.** The target's mode was never persisted; the restart test checked
  the settings and credentials by name. Whole-struct comparisons now fail on
  the next forgotten field.
- **A setting is not a feature.** Coldstart was declared, validated, serialised,
  documented and rendered in a form, and read by nothing that decided
  anything. The cheap check is to search for it in the package that should use
  it. The fix also forced a distinction the engine lacked: a breach no capacity
  can prevent in time asks for what the queue needs once capacity arrives, not
  for the ceiling.
- **A quantity sampled at intervals misses what starts and ends between
  samples.** Short late jobs were never counted; long jobs picked up in time
  were. A breach is now settled when a job starts.
- **Happy-path scenarios never scale down.** Every queue test held capacity
  constant or raised it, and `verify.sh` compared two runs that both lost jobs.

Two experiments found nothing wrong, and are kept for that reason.
**Experiment 003** held the settings store's compare-and-swap under twenty
concurrent editors, with no torn reads and no movement on a rejected write — on
a modest read count, so evidence rather than proof. **Experiment 005** rebuilds
a recorded run from nothing but its provenance, at its recorded commits and Go
toolchain, and replays it identically: 16 metrics, 1,765 cycles, 173 seismic
events, 11 tracks and 2 intent changes, including one made by hand mid-run. It
finds a single altered decision and names it, and refuses a run from a build
with uncommitted changes rather than claim to reproduce code that never ran.

# Threats to validity

- **Few seeds, wide ranges.** Six seeds for the two-hour sweeps, four for the
  calibrated days; exposing events per seed range from 6 to 44, so per-seed
  results disagree widely. The reports print every range.
- **One mine, one workforce model, one array.** Every result is on a 30-sensor
  array; the calibrated days are one mine's pipeline.
- **The two-hour sweeps predate the decay-level fix** and use a job mix that
  could not show it. Re-running them on the calibrated day is outstanding.
- **Exposure is judged at the instant of an event.** The value of restoring work
  for someone who arrives later is not measured, and it is exactly what
  separates final decay from the default.
- **Very high ground motion is almost absent**, so promotion at that level is
  untested.
- **Hazard zones are design upper bounds**, not predictions: C\* = 0.25 and the
  worst radiation direction.
- **The calibration fixes arrivals only.** Deadlines and contention are induced;
  the daily swing, the spread of execution times and the arrival mechanism
  differ from production.
- **Every report here ran on autoscaler 1.x**, which bought the whole cloud cap
  at each cold start and gave cloud back five executors every five minutes
  (findings 005 and 006, found after this brief was first written). Cloud-hour
  figures are inflated by both, and breach counts on the two-hour shape were
  helped by capacity still dripping back when the burst arrived. Comparisons
  between arms of one report share the same autoscaler; absolute cloud hours
  do not carry over to 2.0.
- **Breach counts from before simlab-api 2.0.0 are not comparable in absolute
  terms** (finding 004). Every result in this brief is from 2.0.0 or later.

# Open threads

- **Switch intent on by contention rather than by hand.** It pays under a burst
  or an earthquake and costs on a quiet day, and the platform can already be
  told mid-run; nothing decides it.
- **An allowance from each location's own uncertainty** rather than one number.
- **Promotion of only the next picks an event needs**, not all its remaining work.
- **A measure of the value of restoring decayed work** for someone who arrives
  after an event.
- **More scenario shapes**: other mines, workforces and arrays, and main shocks
  large enough for very high ground motion to occur.
- **Re-run the eight two-hour sweeps on the calibrated day.**
- **Simulate the pipeline end to end** — pick, a timed associate sweep whose
  cost follows what it emits, and locate — so the sweep policy itself becomes a
  parameter under test, with production's fixed 10 s as the control.

sweeps wrote them. Each was produced by both services built from clean
checkouts of the commits shown; rerunning a sweep at those commits reproduces
its `runs.csv` byte for byte, which the digest lets anyone check.

```provenance
```

To regenerate:

```bash
make -C platform-experiments sweep S=NAME             # one report, from sweeps/NAME.json
make -C platform-experiments reproduce RUN=RUN_ID     # one run, rebuilt at its commits
make -C platform-experiments pdf B=2026-09-21-findings  # this document
```

The findings, experiments and calibration notes cited are in
`platform-experiments`: `findings/`, `experiments/`, `docs/calibration.md` and
`docs/workflow-inference.md`. The intent model is specified in simlab-api's
`docs/intent.md`.
